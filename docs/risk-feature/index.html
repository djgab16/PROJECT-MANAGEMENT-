Risk Management & At-Risk Prediction

    The feature · why it exists

    Risk Management for
    Delivery SLAs

    Before

    A coordinator opened the dashboard and saw which deliveries had already breached. The information arrived after the deadline — when the only remaining action was writing an apology.

    After

    Every active delivery carries a risk score, a band, a plain-English reason and a recommended action — recomputed automatically, hours before the deadline.

    The system moved from reporting failure to preventing it.

    What the feature does

    Scope

    It does

    • • Score every active delivery, 0.00 → 1.00
    • • Sort each into Low / Medium / High / Critical
    • • Explain why — a bulleted risk reason per order
    • • Recommend one next action
    • • Alert the floor when an order escalates into Critical
    • • Record what actually happened, then grade itself
    • • Break performance down by route, priority and driver

    It deliberately does not

    • • Reassign or reschedule anything itself. It advises; a human decides.
    • • Call any paid traffic or weather API
    • • Promise a calibrated arrival time
    • • Let the machine-learned model drive operations

    Walk the right-hand column top to bottom — it is the whole feature in one screen. Stress that the output is not just a number: a bare 0.84 is useless to a coordinator at 2am, whereas "no driver assigned, 12 hours left, escalate" is actionable.     How risk is computed

    The core

    Eight signals, each normalised to [0,1], combined by a fixed weight table. The weights sum to exactly 1.00 — enforced by a unit test, so the score can never silently drift out of range.

    The eight risk factors, their weights and scoring rules
    Factor Weight How it scores Why it earns that weight
    Time to deadline 0.35 ≤0h→1.0 · ≤6h→0.9 · ≤12h→0.7
    ≤24h→0.4 · ≤48h→0.2 · else 0    		 The only factor that always gets worse on its own. Nothing predicts a breach better than being nearly out of time.
    Priority 0.15 high 1.0 · medium 0.5
    low 0.1 · unknown 0.3    		 A tighter promise is easier to break. Unknown values score mid-range rather than safe — bad data should not read as low risk.
    Failed attempts 0.15 ≥2→1.0 · 1→0.6 · 0→0.0 A redelivery already failed once. The second attempt is not a fresh start.
    Driver history 0.15 no driver→0.8
    ≥3 past jobs→breach rate
    else 0.2    		 An unassigned order is treated as high risk on purpose — nobody is currently carrying it.
    Route history 0.10 ≥3 past jobs→breach rate
    else 0.2    		 Some routes simply fail more. Requires 3 samples before we trust a rate.
    Package size 0.05 >15kg or >8 items→1.0
    >5kg or >3 items→0.4    		 Bulky loads are slower to handle, but it is a second-order effect.
    Client type 0.03 vip 0.7 · express 0.5
    corporate 0.3 · else 0.1    		 Consequence weighting, not delay prediction. Kept small so it never dominates.
    Operating conditions 0.02 blended history + traffic
    + calendar → slide 4    		 Real, but honestly small. We weighted it by how much we trust it.
    Total 1.00 Unit-tested with rounding — values like 0.35 and 0.03 are not exactly representable in binary floating point.

    Speaker notes

    This is the slide you will be questioned on. Do not read all eight — say "time to deadline carries a third of the weight, because it is the only signal that always gets worse on its own," then jump to the two design decisions worth defending: an unassigned order scores 0.8, and unknown priority scores 0.3, not 0 — missing data must never read as safe. If asked "where did the weights come from?": they are expert-assigned, not fitted, which is exactly why we built the shadow model on slide 7 to test them.

    From score to decision

    Bands

    ≥ 0.80

    Critical

    Escalate now. Fires an alert on entry to this band.

    ≥ 0.60

    High

    Intervene today — assign, reassign or contact.

    ≥ 0.35

    Medium

    Monitor closely. Still counts as at-risk.

    < 0.35

    Low

    No action required.

    What “at risk” means — precisely

    isAtRisk = score ≥ 0.35  OR  deadline already passed

    Two things worth noting. First, the flag reads the same constant as the Medium band rather than a copied literal — so retuning the band can never silently decouple the flag from the label. Second, a breached order is flagged regardless of score; there is no arithmetic route to missing an order that is already late.

    Confidence

    Starts at 0.60 and rises as real history backs the score — driver record, route record, parsed weight, client record.

    Capped at 0.98. It is a prediction; it never claims certainty.

    Known quirk, stated up front: because the client-type factor tops out at 0.7, the maximum reachable score is about 0.99, not 1.00. Nothing in the system assumes a perfect 1.0 is attainable.

    Speaker notes

    The four cards are self-explanatory — do not narrate them. Spend the time on the isAtRisk line, especially the OR deadline passed clause: it guarantees a late order can never be scored into invisibility. Then volunteer the 0.99 ceiling. It is a small, precise, self-caught flaw, and disclosing it buys you far more credibility than it costs.

    The factor we had to rebuild

    Deep dive

    What we found

    The operating-conditions factor had hardcoded traffic and weather values. It resolved to one of two numbers — 0.2 on a weekday, 0.533 on a weekend.

    weighted spread = 0.533 − 0.2 × 0.02
                     = 0.0067

    The narrowest band gap is 0.25. This factor was mathematically incapable of moving a single order between bands. It was decoration.

    What we replaced it with

    Our own delivery history — no paid API, no new dependency. Three blended signals whose weights also sum to 1.00:

    0.55
    Historical breach rate for this route, area and time of day
    0.25
    Congestion — Metro Manila rush windows, 7–9am and 5–7pm
    0.20
    Calendar — weekend versus weekday

    The fallback chain — a new route still gets a defensible number

    Each level needs at least 3 past deliveries before we trust it. If it doesn't, we fall through:

    1. route + time
    2. →
    3. area + time
    4. →
    5. route overall
    6. →
    7. area overall
    8. →
    9. global
    10. →
    11. 0.2 default

    Also worth noting: conditions are evaluated against the delivery slot, not the moment we happen to run — because what matters is the traffic at 6pm Friday, not at 10am when the scorer fired.

    Speaker notes

    This is your strongest technical slide — you audited your own model and proved a component was inert. Deliver the arithmetic slowly: "0.0067 of weighted movement, against a smallest band gap of 0.25." Then the payoff line: "it looked like it was working, and it could not possibly have been." The fallback chain answers the obvious follow-up — "what about a brand-new route?" — so let them ask it if you are short on time.

    Running it unattended

    Automation

    A score that only updates when somebody clicks a button is wrong by design — the heaviest factor decays with the clock. So it runs itself.

    Every 30 minutes, guarded

    • • Hard 5-minute floor — a bad config value cannot make it hot-spin
    • • Overlapping runs are skipped, not queued — no pile-up under load
    • • Errors caught inside the loop — one bad record cannot kill the service
    • • 60-second startup delay — lets the app finish booting first

    One pass, zero N+1

    All driver, route and client history plus the conditions snapshot are fetched once per run. Inside the scoring loop there is no database access at all — scoring 500 orders costs the same handful of queries as scoring 5.

    Separable audit trail

    Scheduled runs log under AutoRun-, operator-triggered runs under Run-. You can always tell whether a human or the clock produced a score.

    Alerting without alert fatigue

    The naive version re-alerts on every Critical order, every run. That is 48 pages a day for one delivery — and a floor that learns to ignore the system.

    The fix

    Read the previous band before overwriting it, and alert only on the transition into Critical.

    High → Critical  =  alert

    Critical → Critical  =  silence

    Alerts land in the existing notification feed, tagged SLA Prediction Engine, carrying the recommended action as the body — so the alert itself tells the coordinator what to do.

    Speaker notes

    The "48 pages a day" number is what makes this slide memorable — use it. The transition-detection fix is a genuinely non-obvious engineering insight, and it shows you thought about the human on the receiving end, not just the algorithm. If someone asks about scale, the N+1 panel is your answer: constant query count regardless of order volume.

    But is it actually right?

    Validation

    The trap we avoided

    Our completed-deliveries view derives its risk fields from the outcome — a breached order is labelled High after the fact. Measuring accuracy against that view returns 100%, truthfully computed, and completely worthless.

    What we do instead

    When a delivery reaches a terminal status, we snapshot the prediction as it stood before the result was known, alongside what actually happened.

    • • Features are copied verbatim, never recomputed — recomputing time-based factors after delivery would leak the answer into its own inputs
    • • Written in the same transaction as the status change
    • • Idempotent — three layers of guard, including a unique index
    • • If no prediction existed, the order is skipped, not invented

    The confusion matrix, in operator language

    TP

    Correctly flagged at risk

    FP

    False alarms

    TN

    Correctly cleared

    FN

    Missed breaches

    Reported as accuracy, precision, recall and F1 — and rendered on the dashboard with those plain-English labels, because "false negative" means nothing to a coordinator but "missed breaches" does.

    With no outcomes yet, the endpoint returns an explicit “not yet validated” state — never a measured zero.

    Speaker notes

    This is the most important slide in the deck. Any team can show a risk score; almost none can show they refused a flattering metric. Deliver the line and then stop talking for a beat: "we could have reported 100% accuracy, computed correctly, and it would have meant nothing." Explaining why you rejected it demonstrates more understanding than any number you could put on screen. Have the live figures ready but do not lead with them.

    Testing the weights with a real model

    Shadow mode

    Our eight weights are expert-assigned, not fitted. The honest question is “would a model trained on our own outcomes choose differently?” So we built one — and gave it no authority whatsoever.

    What it is

    Logistic regression, written directly in C# — no ML framework. The eight factors are already normalised, so no scaling was needed and a heavyweight dependency would have bought us nothing.

    Fully deterministic — same data in, identical coefficients out, every time. That is what lets us unit-test a trained model.

    What “shadow” means

    It writes to three separate nullable columns. The score, the band and the at-risk flag — the fields the dashboard, the alerts and the summary all read — are never touched by it.

    It ships switched off. Installing the build changes nothing until somebody deliberately enables it.

    Refusing to train

    Four guards. It declines rather than fitting nonsense:

    • • All-breach or all-safe data — would learn to say one word forever
    • • Too few rows to identify 8 coefficients
    • • Out-of-range rows dropped, not clamped — clamping trains on a value that never existed
    • • Non-finite input rejected before it can poison every coefficient

    Each refusal reports why to the operator.

    A fair head-to-head

    Both scorers are graded on exactly the same deliveries — only rows the model actually scored — through the same metric code, in one pass. Neither side can be credited for a row the other never saw.

    The headline is F1 delta: positive means the model is ahead. F1 rather than raw accuracy, because accuracy rewards a model that just predicts the majority class.

    How to talk about it

    “Our live risk scores are 100% rule-based. We additionally trained a logistic-regression challenger that runs alongside in shadow, so we can prove whether a fitted model would do better before we would ever trust one.”

    Do not call the rule-based engine AI. Do not claim the model drives decisions. It has to earn promotion, and it has not been promoted.

    Speaker notes

    Frame the model as a test of the weights, not as a headline feature — that framing is both accurate and much harder to attack. Read the scripted sentence in the red box close to verbatim; it is written to be challenge-proof. Expect "why not just use the ML model?" — the answer is that it has not been validated on enough real outcomes yet, and shipping an unvalidated model into an operational alerting path would be indefensible.

    Every 30 seconds, by polling rather than sockets — a deliberate reliability choice after our socket layer kept dropping connections.

    Operations team and above. Drivers and clients are blocked at both the route and the API — demonstrable live, and it reuses the existing policy rather than inventing a new one.

    What it can't do yet

    Honest limits

    Every one of these is documented in our source code rather than discovered by a reviewer. We are telling you because a disclosed limitation costs a fraction of a discovered one.

    Predicted arrival is directional, not calibrated

    The arrival estimate adds a hand-picked offset per risk band. Those four numbers were not fitted from historical delay data. It is useful for sorting a worklist and must not be quoted to a customer as an ETA. Fitting it from the observed delay distribution is the clearest next step.

    The breach counter mixes scope

    It spans archived and active orders, while the active-deliveries count excludes archived. Almost certainly unintended — but the figure was already published in our Week 5 documentation, so we reproduced it verbatim and documented the flaw rather than silently changing a number under review.

    The weights are judgement, not evidence

    Expert-assigned and defensible, but not derived from our data. The shadow model exists precisely to answer this, and it needs more recorded outcomes before its verdict means anything.

    Smaller known gaps

    • • The at-risk KPI label reads “High / Critical”, but the underlying flag also includes Medium — a copy fix, not a logic one
    • • The maximum reachable score is about 0.99, not 1.00
    • • The learned model has no user interface — it is reachable by API only
    • • Backend is well covered by tests; the frontend has no test runner wired up

    Speaker notes

    Counter-intuitively this is a strong slide, so do not rush it apologetically. Say the framing line out loud: "documented in our source, not discovered by a reviewer." The arrival-offset admission is the one that matters most — if a panel member were going to catch anything, it would be that, and volunteering it converts a potential ambush into evidence of rigour. Pair each limit with its next step so the slide reads as a roadmap, not a confession.
